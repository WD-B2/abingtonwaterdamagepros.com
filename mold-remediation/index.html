Is it mold? Spots, smells and damp closets

Most mold calls start with a symptom and not a diagnosis. A dark patch at the corner of a bathroom ceiling, a musty smell under the basement stairs, spots on a closet wall or a carpet corner that looks wrong after a leak all raise the same question.

You cannot tell mold from other staining by looking, and only an assessment or lab test confirms it. What you can tell is whether moisture is present, because mold needs it. A recent leak from a toilet, sink, air conditioner condensate line, roof or siding is the most common starting point.

If you see growth, do not disturb it, brush it or paint over it. Photograph it, note where the water came from and call for an assessment. Health questions about mold belong with a physician.

Mold needs moisture, so the water source comes first

Mold remediation cannot succeed while the leak continues. The first step is to find and fix the moisture source, whether that is a plumbing leak, a clogged condensate line, a roof opening or condensation in a closet.

Drying follows, because mold grows on damp surfaces and stalls when they are dry. Remediation then removes the growth and contaminated materials. No company can promise that mold will never return, because new moisture can start it again.

Our AC leak water damage page covers condensate lines, and the ceiling water damage repair page covers upstairs leaks that run through ceilings into finished basements.

What a mold assessment looks at

An assessment starts with the building, not a lab kit. A technician looks at where the water came from, what materials are wet and how far growth extends, with a moisture meter and a thermal camera.

Visual inspection

Walls, ceilings, closets, under sinks, behind furniture and around the source.

Moisture readings

Meters show whether the area is still damp and whether the source is active.

Sampling

Air or surface samples can be taken to confirm and identify growth if needed.

Scope of work

A written plan lists areas, materials to remove and containment.

Callers sometimes ask for independent clearance testing, which means a third party checks that the work succeeded. Clearance testing is a separate step from the work itself, and an independent tester avoids a conflict of interest. Ask any contractor how it handles verification.

How remediation works

Remediation follows the ANSI/IICRC S520 standard for mold remediation, which you can ask any company about. The steps depend on how much area is involved, and larger jobs use more containment.

  1. 1

    Contain

    Plastic sheeting and negative air machines keep spores from spreading.

  2. 2

    Protect

    Crews wear protective gear and cover vents and nearby surfaces.

  3. 3

    Remove

    Porous materials with growth, such as drywall and carpet, are cut out and bagged. Non-porous surfaces are cleaned.

  4. 4

    Clean

    HEPA vacuums and damp wiping remove residue from framing and surfaces.

  5. 5

    Dry and verify

    The area is dried, and clearance or visual verification confirms the work.

Bleach and over-the-counter sprays are not a substitute. They can make a surface look cleaner while leaving the cause and the growth inside the material.

Testing, selling a home and lenders

Real estate calls are common. A buyer's lender may require mold to be addressed before it will fund a loan, and a seller may need proof that the work was done. Independent clearance testing can provide a record, and requirements vary by lender and by buyer.

We cannot promise any lender or buyer will accept a result. Ask your agent or lender what paperwork they need before work starts. Our water extraction page covers the water side of a leak that led to mold, and our structural drying page explains drying.

What affects the cost

Mold remediation is priced by the area affected, the materials that must be removed, the amount of containment, any testing and the rebuild. A small localized patch is a smaller scope than growth spread across several rooms.

We explain the scope and the price before work starts, in writing, and a call is the quickest way to get a number for your property. Insurance coverage varies, and mold that follows a sudden covered leak may be treated differently from mold caused by long-term moisture. Read your policy.

State rules on mold work vary, so ask whether your state licenses mold work and ask any contractor to explain its credentials in writing.

Questions about mold remediation

How do I know if it is mold?

Only an assessment or lab test confirms it. Dark spots, a musty smell and a recent leak are reasons to have the area checked.

Can I remove mold myself with bleach?

Bleach does not solve the moisture problem and can leave growth inside porous materials. Small non-porous surface spots are different from growth in drywall or carpet, so ask a professional.

What is independent clearance testing?

A third party checks that the area meets the cleanliness standard after remediation. It is separate from the removal work, which avoids a conflict of interest.

How quickly do you need to come out for an inspection?

Call and describe the situation. Same-day response is offered when crews are available, and dispatch will tell you what to do in the meantime.

Will the mold come back?

No contractor can promise that. If moisture returns, mold can return, so fixing the source and keeping the area dry matter most.
