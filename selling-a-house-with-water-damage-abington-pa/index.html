Selling a House With Water Damage in Abington, PA

Water damage does not end a sale, but hiding it can. This guide covers what Pennsylvania requires you to disclose and how repairs and records help.

  • By the Abington Water Damage Pros team
  • Published November 12, 2026

The short answer

In Pennsylvania, sellers must disclose known material defects on the state's property disclosure form, and the form asks about basements and crawl spaces, water and sewage systems and the roof. Known water intrusion that significantly affects value or creates an unreasonable risk is the kind of thing to disclose. You can repair before selling, sell as is or credit the buyer, but you cannot hide a known problem.

This is general information from the statute and not legal advice. Ask a real estate attorney or your agent about your situation.

What the Pennsylvania law requires

The Pennsylvania Real Estate Seller Disclosure Law requires a seller to disclose known material defects before signing an agreement of transfer. A material defect is a problem that has a significant adverse impact on the value of the property or that involves an unreasonable risk to people on the property.

Key points of the seller disclosure law
PointWhat it means for a seller
Known material defects must be disclosedDisclose water problems you know about

The law has other details, including remedies and time limits, and the statute is published by the state. Read it or ask an attorney before relying on a summary.

Repair before you list, or sell as is

Both paths can work, and the right one depends on the damage and the market.

Repair first

You control the work and the records, and buyers see a finished house. Costs come first and the timing may be longer.

Sell as is

You list the house with the damage disclosed. Buyers may offer less, and lenders may require repairs before closing.

Credit or price adjustment

You disclose and offer a credit, leaving the repair to the buyer.

Partial work

You do mitigation and drying now and leave cosmetic repairs for the buyer.

Drying is often the sensible first step regardless of the plan. Wet materials get worse each day and can grow mold, which can turn a small repair into a bigger disclosure. Our structural drying page explains how professionals verify that a structure is dry.

Mold and lender requirements

Mold shows up often in sales. A buyer's lender may refuse to fund a loan until visible mold is addressed, and a buyer may ask for proof that the work was done. Independent clearance testing, which is a third-party check after remediation, can provide that record.

We cannot promise any lender will accept a result, so ask the lender what it needs. Our mold remediation page explains the process, and the standard crews follow is ANSI/IICRC S520.

Document everything

Paperwork matters more in a sale than in a typical repair. Keep records that show what happened and what was done.

  1. 1

    Cause and date

    Note what caused the water and when it was found.

  2. 2

    Mitigation records

    Keep the moisture readings, photos and drying logs a crew provides.

  3. 3

    Repair invoices

    Keep written scopes, receipts and permits for the rebuild.

  4. 4

    Insurance papers

    Keep the claim number and any settlement letters.

  5. 5

    Disclosure form

    Complete it honestly and keep a signed copy.

A buyer's inspector may find water stains, soft spots or a musty smell, and a clear paper trail makes those questions easier to answer.

Permits and records locally

Permits can be part of the paper trail for a repair. Abington Township's Community Development Department at 1176 Old York Road handles building, zoning, plumbing and HVAC permits, and Jenkintown says permits are required before any work and are decided within 15 business days. Ask the office that covers your address which repairs need one, and keep the permit and inspection records with your sale documents.

What buyers and inspectors look for

Inspectors look for stains on ceilings and walls, soft or discolored flooring, musty odors, efflorescence on basement walls and signs of past repairs. A freshly painted patch on a ceiling is a clue, and so is a basement with new drywall and no history.

If your house had a leak, it is better to disclose the cause, the repair and the proof than to hope no one notices. Our ceiling water damage repair and basement water damage pages explain the two places buyers usually look first.

Pricing and negotiation

A disclosed, repaired problem is easier to price than an unknown one. If you cannot afford repairs before listing, a documented estimate gives a buyer a number to work from, and many buyers prefer that to a guess.

Talk with your agent about whether to repair, credit or adjust the price. We do not predict how a buyer or lender will respond.

Selling in nearby boroughs

Different towns have different permit rules and housing, so check locally. If your home is in Jenkintown or Hatboro, see our pages on Jenkintown and Hatboro.

Questions about selling a house with water damage in Abington

Do I have to disclose water damage that was already fixed?

The form asks about known problems, so ask your agent or attorney how to describe a past issue and its repair. Keep the records that show it was fixed.

Can I sell the house as is?

Often, yes. You still must disclose known material defects, and a buyer's lender may require repairs before closing.

Should I dry the house before listing?

Drying early prevents more damage and mold, and the records can help in the sale.

What if a buyer finds mold during inspection?

Expect questions about the source and the repair. Mold remediation with documentation, and sometimes independent clearance testing, can help answer them.
